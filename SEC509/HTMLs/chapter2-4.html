Cybersecurity Culture & Human Behaviour

Understanding the human elements that shape organizational and personal cybersecurity

Human Factors in Cybersecurity

Introduction to Human Factors in Cybersecurity

While much of cybersecurity focuses on technology—firewalls, encryption, software patches—the human element is equally, if not more, critical to security. "Human factors in cybersecurity" refers to how human behavior, psychology, and organizational culture influence security outcomes.

Why human factors matter: Studies consistently show that the majority of security breaches involve human error or deliberate human actions. No amount of advanced technology can compensate for poor security practices, lack of awareness, or a culture where security is treated as an inconvenience rather than a responsibility.

Key aspects of human factors include:

  • Security awareness: How well individuals understand security risks and best practices. Many people don't realize they're vulnerable until they experience a breach.
  • Decision-making under pressure: When faced with time pressure or competing priorities, people often make poor security choices (reusing passwords, sharing credentials, skipping verification steps).
  • Risk perception: People often underestimate security risks that seem unlikely or distant. "It won't happen to me" is a common attitude that leads to dangerous behavior.
  • Compliance vs. security: There's a difference between following security policies because you must and genuinely understanding why policies exist. Forced compliance without understanding creates resentment.
  • Social dynamics: Trust, authority, and group pressure influence behavior. People are more likely to fall for social engineering if it comes from someone in authority or a trusted peer.
  • Motivation: People are more likely to follow security practices if they understand the "why" and see security as their responsibility, not something imposed on them.

The paradox of cybersecurity: Effective security often requires friction—multi-factor authentication adds a step, strong passwords are hard to remember, checking email links takes time. People naturally resist friction, creating a constant tension between usability and security. Organizations must find the right balance.

Building human-centered security: Rather than assuming people will follow perfect practices, effective security acknowledges human limitations and designs systems and cultures that support secure behavior. This includes training, clear policies, technology that makes security easier, and leadership that models secure practices.

Human Failures, Errors, and Violations

Not all human security breaches are the same. Understanding the differences between failures, errors, and violations helps organizations and individuals address root causes more effectively.

Errors (Unintentional mistakes):

  • What they are: Mistakes made despite good intentions and genuine effort to follow security practices. The person knows what they should do but accidentally does something different.
  • Examples:
    • Sending a confidential email to the wrong recipient (typing "john.smith@company.com" instead of "john.smith@partner.com")
    • Accidentally opening a phishing email attachment because the sender name looked familiar
    • Leaving a computer unlocked and walking away
    • Misconfiguring security settings
    • Forgetting to verify a sender's email address before responding to a request
  • Root causes: Fatigue, distraction, confusion, lack of clear procedures, or inadequate training.
  • How to address errors: Improve training clarity, reduce cognitive load, add safeguards (confirmation dialogs, double-check requirements), improve interface design, and address fatigue through reasonable workloads.

Failures (Lack of knowledge or capability):

  • What they are: Breaches that occur because a person doesn't know what the correct procedure is or doesn't have the capability to perform it. They aren't doing anything intentionally wrong—they simply don't know better.
  • Examples:
    • Using weak passwords because they don't understand why strong passwords matter
    • Sharing credentials with a colleague because they don't know how credential sharing violates policy
    • Clicking suspicious links because they can't recognize phishing attempts
    • Downloading files from untrusted sources because they don't understand malware risks
    • Connecting personal devices to work networks without understanding the security implications
  • Root causes: Inadequate training, unclear policies, lack of security awareness, or insufficient experience.
  • How to address failures: Comprehensive security training and awareness programs, clear documentation of policies and procedures, mentoring, hands-on workshops, and regular refreshers as threats evolve.

Violations (Intentional non-compliance):

  • What they are: Deliberate decisions to ignore security policies or best practices. The person knows what they should do but chooses not to do it.
  • Examples:
    • Deliberately using a weak, memorable password instead of following the strong password policy
    • Sharing login credentials because it's faster than going through access request procedures
    • Disabling antivirus software because it slows down work
    • Writing down passwords on sticky notes despite knowing it's against policy
    • Using personal USB drives to copy work files, violating data protection policies
    • Accessing files or systems outside of job responsibilities out of curiosity
  • Root causes: Competing priorities and time pressure, perceived unnecessary restrictions, inconvenience of security measures, lack of accountability, low risk perception, or even intentional malice (insider threats).
  • How to address violations: Make policies realistic and not overly burdensome, clearly explain the "why" behind policies, provide feedback about security incidents, establish consistent accountability, lead by example from management, and create a culture where security is valued, not resented.

Important distinction: Errors and failures deserve training and support—punishing someone for accidentally clicking a phishing link is counterproductive. However, intentional violations may require different approaches, including clear consequences. Organizations that punish all security incidents equally (whether errors, failures, or violations) often create a culture where people hide incidents rather than reporting them, making security worse overall.

The Role of Security Culture in Organizations

Security culture is the collective attitude, beliefs, and practices of an organization toward security. It's the difference between a workplace where security is everyone's responsibility and one where it's seen as someone else's job.

Characteristics of strong security cultures:

  • Leadership commitment: Security is visibly prioritized by senior leadership, not just delegated to the IT department.
  • Shared responsibility: Everyone understands they have a role in security, not just IT specialists.
  • Psychological safety: People feel safe reporting security incidents and near-misses without fear of punishment for honest mistakes.
  • Continuous learning: Regular training, awareness campaigns, and discussions about threats keep security top-of-mind.
  • Incentives aligned with security: Performance evaluations and rewards consider security practices, not just productivity.
  • Transparency about incidents: Organizations communicate about security events and lessons learned to improve collectively.
  • User-centered security: Policies and tools are designed with usability in mind, not as obstacles to work.

Weak security cultures: In contrast, organizations with poor security culture often have widespread policy violations, little reporting of incidents (because people fear consequences), outdated training, policies that contradict actual work practices, and a view of security as a burden rather than an asset.

Social Engineering and Manipulation

Social engineering exploits human psychology and trust rather than technical vulnerabilities. It's often the easiest path to breaching security because people are fundamentally cooperative and want to help.

Common social engineering tactics:

  • Pretext: Creating a fabricated scenario to manipulate someone into revealing information. "I'm from IT, I need to verify your password to troubleshoot your account."
  • Authority: Impersonating someone with authority to bypass normal procedures. "This is the CEO; I need access to the quarterly reports immediately."
  • Urgency: Creating time pressure that prevents careful thinking. "Your account has been compromised; click this link immediately to verify."
  • Scarcity: Suggesting limited opportunity. "This offer expires today; you must act now."
  • Reciprocity: Building a sense of obligation. "I helped you last week; can you do me a favor and access these files for me?"
  • Trust and likeability: Building rapport and appearing trustworthy before making the ask. Attackers research targets to find commonalities and shared contacts.
  • Fear: Threatening consequences for non-compliance. "If you don't provide access, your department will be audited."

Why social engineering works: People are trained to be helpful and to trust colleagues. Security policies often feel arbitrary compared to the immediate human need being presented. Most people want to cooperate and feel uncomfortable refusing requests, especially from authority figures.

Defense against social engineering: Verification procedures (always verify identity through known contact methods), training on common tactics, awareness of urgency and authority pressure as red flags, clear escalation paths for unusual requests, and a culture where it's acceptable to say "no" or "let me verify this."

Motivation and Behavior Change in Security

Understanding what motivates people to follow security practices is crucial for designing effective security programs. Motivation research shows that compliance based on fear or punishment is often short-lived and superficial.

Types of motivation:

  • Intrinsic motivation: People follow security practices because they genuinely understand why it matters and value the outcome. This is the most sustainable form of motivation.
  • Extrinsic motivation: People follow practices because of external rewards or punishment (bonuses, penalties, surveillance). This motivation disappears when external rewards are removed.

Building intrinsic motivation for security:

  • Explain the "why": Help people understand how security practices protect them personally, protect the organization's mission, and protect colleagues.
  • Make it relatable: Use real stories of how breaches affected people, not abstract statistics.
  • Involve people in decision-making: When people help create security policies, they're more likely to follow them.
  • Celebrate successes: Recognize and reward good security practices, not just punish failures.
  • Remove friction where possible: If security practices are overly burdensome, people will find workarounds. Invest in user-friendly security tools.
  • Create community: Make security a shared value and identity, not an imposed requirement.

Risk Perception and Decision-Making

How people perceive security risks significantly influences their behavior. Interestingly, risk perception often doesn't match actual risk.

Factors that influence risk perception:

  • Familiarity: Risks that are familiar feel less dangerous. Using the same weak password everywhere feels normal and therefore safe, even though it's actually risky.
  • Personal experience: People who have experienced a breach are much more likely to take security seriously than those who haven't. Hypothetical risks feel abstract and unlikely.
  • Visibility: Invisible risks are underestimated. Malware that silently steals data feels less real than a virus that crashes your computer.
  • Dread: Risks that feel catastrophic are overestimated. Fear of dramatic breaches sometimes overshadows the more common, mundane risks.
  • Optimism bias: "It won't happen to me" is a common bias. People think security risks apply to others, not themselves.
  • Status quo bias: People stick with current practices even when better alternatives exist, because change feels risky.

Implications for security programs: Simply telling people "don't click suspicious links" isn't effective when they don't perceive the risk as real or relevant. Effective security education helps people understand risks in concrete, relevant terms and provides tools and processes that make secure behavior the path of least resistance.
